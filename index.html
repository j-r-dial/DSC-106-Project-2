Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Hannah Huang, Romy Ramrakhyani, Julia Dial — @hhh007@ucsd.edu, rramrakhyani@ucsd.edu, jrdial@ucsd.edu

Proposition

FOR the Proposition: The Geographic access to abortion across US states is deeply unequal and unfair depending on your Geographic location.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Stoplight Color Encoding (Maroon to Green) - Score: 1.5: We used high contrast colors, maroon for bans and green for protection, to tap into universal psychological associations with "stop" and "go." This choice is earnest because it accurately reflects the physical reality of a person being able to access care or being legally halted. We considered using a single color gradient, but it failed to capture the categorical "cliff" that exists when crossing state lines, which is central to our proposition.
  • Ordered Legend Hierarchy - Score: 1.0: The legend is forced into a logical progression from "Total Ban" to "No Gestational Limit" rather than being sorted alphabetically. This helps the viewer understand the systemic nature of the disparity by framing the data as a spectrum of rights. Alphabetical sorting was an alternative, but it felt neutral and obscured the effect we was trying to highlight.
  • Data Categorization (Specific Week Limits) - Score: 0.5: Instead of a simple "legal vs. illegal" binary, we transformed the data to show specific week based bans (6-week, 12-week, etc.). This choice mostly earnest because it gives viewers a more detailed understanding of how access differs across states. It also is persuasive because it reveals that even states where abortion is technically legal, they may still have major restrictions making geographical inequity more visible. I initially thought about using fewer categories, but decided that being more specific about the week limits made the "inequality" argument feel more grounded in fact.

AGAINST the Proposition: Geographic access to abortion care varies by state, but it's not as dramatic as it seems.

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Scaling: One intentional design decision was adjusting the scaling of the number of clinics per 1,000 square feet so that there was uniformity in the colors across states. This decision would be a -2 on the deception scale, as it was done to decieve the viewer.
  • Normalizing by state area: We normalized the number of clinics by state area to compute clinics per square unit (e.g., per 1,000 square miles). This decision is a 2 on the deceptive scale because it is earnest in allowing more fair and meaningful comparisons of clinic density across states. This ensures that larger states do not appear artificially having more access to abortion clinics simply because they cover more land.
  • Using a choropleth map: This choice of visual mark, over using a bar chart or other type of mark, allows us to visualize geographic variation in clinic density across states. This design makes regional patterns visible, especially allowing for viewers to quickly identify regional disparities. We would score this a -0.5, because choropleth maps can obscure within-state variation by uniformly coloring entire states. However, this isn’t entirely deceptive for our visualization as we are looking to compare larger regional access to abortion rather than within-state variation.

Final Reflection

The process of designing visualizations for both sides of the abortion access proposition revealed to us that truth in data is often a matter of perspective and framing. The most straightforward part of the process was creating our earnest visualizations, as the raw data from the Guttmacher Institute naturally supported our narrative of inequality through its extreme outliers. However, the most difficult aspect was the deceptive phase, intentionally stripping away geographic and demographic context to make a disparate system look uniform. We were surprised to see how easily a logarithmic scale or a wide Y-axis could flatten a crisis, effectively hiding thousands of miles of travel or hundreds of clinic closures under a veneer of statistical stability.

We now define ethical analysis and visualization not as the absence of persuasion, but as the commitment to preserving the functional reality of the human experience behind the data. In our view, an earnest visualization provides the viewer with the necessary context, such as population density or literal week counts, to understand the real-world impact of a policy. In contrast, we found that deceptive visualization relies on statistical smoothing, where regional averaging or mismatched scales are used to intentionally obscure the challenges faced by individuals. While we recognize that all visualization requires some level of data reduction, we believe an ethical approach ensures that this reduction does not result in a total loss of the data's original meaning.

The acceptable bounds we have drawn for persuasive choice lie in the transparency of the transformation. We believe it is acceptable to use a stoplight color palette to highlight a ban, as that reflects the legal reality of the situation; however, we consider it "misleading" when we use a 0–100 scale to represent a dataset that never exceeds 15. This type of scale manipulation is a lie of omission, as it uses the white space of the graph to trick the eye into seeing insignificance. Ultimately, we concluded that a visualization remains ethical as long as it empowers the viewer to make an informed decision, whereas it becomes misleading the moment it uses design as a tool to hide the very evidence it claims to present.
